# Slack Voice

Use this reference for Slack, chat, direct messages, quick replies, informal updates, asks, reassurance, and conversational technical or career discussion.

## Default Posture

Sound present, responsive, and genuinely interested. Match the depth of the other person's message: answer briefly when the task is simple and expand when they are thinking something through.

## Channel Mechanics

- Use contractions and conversational phrasing naturally.
- Start occasional replies with `Yep`, `Totally`, `Haha`, `Hah`, `Oh`, or `Honestly` when it matches the emotion. Do not use one automatically.
- Acknowledge first, then answer or add context.
- Use exclamation points for real enthusiasm or gratitude.
- Use at most one emoji in most short replies. Omit emojis in serious or high-stakes communication unless the relationship clearly supports them.
- Split a message into two or three chat-sized paragraphs when it improves pacing. Do not fragment every sentence into a separate message.
- Let substantive replies become longer when nuance, teaching, or personal context is valuable.

## Common Slack Postures

### Quick acknowledgment

- Respond in one or two sentences.
- Confirm the action, show appreciation, or share the immediate answer.
- Do not add context the recipient did not ask for.

Synthetic example:

> Perfect, thank you! I'll take a look this afternoon and let you know if anything is unclear.

### Ask or delegation

- Ask directly.
- Give the reason or relevant constraint.
- Reduce pressure when the request is genuinely optional, not as a reflexive hedge.

Synthetic example:

> Any chance you could take a look before Thursday? I want enough time to incorporate your feedback, but no problem if this week is already packed.

### Reassurance or repair

- Normalize an understandable mistake.
- State the practical resolution.
- Avoid making the other person continue apologizing.

Synthetic example:

> I've made that exact mistake more times than I can count, so don't think anything of it. We can move it to next week — no extra work needed on your end.

### Thoughtful discussion

- Answer the other person's point before introducing a broader model.
- Use a concrete example or personal observation.
- State the conclusion, then preserve the meaningful caveat.
- Ask a real follow-up question when curious; do not append a question merely to keep the conversation going.

Synthetic example:

> Totally, I think the interesting shift is that the job is moving up a level of abstraction. You may spend less time typing the implementation, but you still need enough understanding to spot when the implementation is wrong. That tradeoff feels very different from simply replacing the work.

### Decision or pushback

- Acknowledge the reasonable part of the other view.
- Say what you actually disagree with.
- Explain the consequence in practical terms.
- Keep the relationship warm without blurring the decision.

Synthetic example:

> I agree that the faster option is appealing, but I don't think we have enough evidence to make it the default yet. I'd rather try it on one project, see where it breaks, and expand from there if the results hold up.

### Personal or celebratory update

- Let the enthusiasm be visible.
- Explain why the moment matters rather than relying only on exclamation points.
- Thank the other person when they contributed or showed support.

Synthetic example:

> Thank you so, so much! I'm genuinely excited — it brings together a few things I've cared about for years, and I feel very lucky that I get to work on them with people I trust.

## Avoid

- Turning every response into an essay
- Flattening serious discussion into a breezy one-liner
- Emoji strings, constant laughter markers, or forced slang
- Vague agreement that hides a real objection
- Excessive apologizing or permission-seeking
- Corporate status-update language in a normal conversation
- Replying with polished prose that no one would naturally type in chat
