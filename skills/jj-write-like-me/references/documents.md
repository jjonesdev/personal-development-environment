# Document Voice

Use this reference for articles, essays, public announcements, talks, educational material, and other long-form prose.

## Default Posture

Write as an experienced practitioner explaining something important to an intelligent reader. Teach without lecturing. Make a real argument, show the reasoning, and stay accessible to readers with different levels of expertise.

## Structure

1. Open with a clear hook: a timely observation, candid claim, compact story, or surprising contrast.
2. State the thesis early enough that the reader knows where the piece is going.
3. Break the body into descriptive sections that advance the argument.
4. Explain abstractions through concrete examples, comparisons, small scenarios, or numbers.
5. Address the strongest relevant caveat instead of pretending the argument has no limits.
6. Finish with a concise implication, principle, or invitation to think differently.

Use headings for genuine changes in the argument. Do not create a heading for every paragraph.

## Paragraph and Sentence Shape

- Give each paragraph one main job.
- Alternate exposition with examples so the piece does not become abstract.
- Use occasional one-sentence paragraphs for emphasis.
- Build causal connections explicitly with words such as `because`, `but`, `so`, and `that means`.
- Use parallel structure for steps or contrasts.
- Prefer an active first-person voice when personal experience is relevant.
- Keep the prose conversational even when the subject is technical.

## Common Document Postures

### Public announcement

- Lead with the news and genuine enthusiasm.
- Explain what the reader will get from it.
- Add one light, situational joke only if natural.
- End with a direct link or clear next action.

### Educational explainer

- Establish why the topic matters before teaching mechanics.
- Move from familiar experience to the underlying model.
- Define technical ideas with practical language and examples.
- Make limitations visible; do not oversell the tool or technique.

### Argument or opinion

- State a defensible thesis.
- Name the apparent contradiction or common explanation.
- Offer a clearer causal model.
- Test it against examples and acknowledge human consequences.
- End with the real takeaway, even when there is no tidy solution.

## Synthetic Examples

### Opening

> The impressive part is not that the tool produced an answer in thirty seconds. The important part is whether that answer survives thirty minutes of scrutiny.

### Nuanced transition

> That does not make the tool useless — far from it. It means the speed of producing work has changed faster than our habits for judging the work, and those two things need to catch up with each other.

### Conclusion

> You are still responsible for what reaches another person. The tools may change how quickly you get there, but they do not change that obligation.

## Avoid

- Academic throat-clearing before the thesis
- A neutral survey when the piece calls for a position
- Hype without mechanics or evidence
- Technical jargon used as a substitute for explanation
- Excessive listicles, slogans, or clickbait
- A polished conclusion that evades the argument's uncomfortable implication
